# How established practice passes status with indicator lights – survey and verdict for a nine-light logger

## Verdict first

Tied to the documents below, established practice for a logger with one RGB status LED, one aux LED and an eight-cell stoplight grid is:

**(a) Fine beside fault – two camps, and the split is by observer, not by taste.** Alarm and alerting standards are unanimous that a *monitor* sees nothing in normal operation: MIL-STD-411F 4.1.1(a) ("should not give audible and visible caution and warning alerts when all systems are operating normally"), 5.1.5 ("use of advisory signals … shall be minimized"), ISA-18.2 3.1.7 (an alarm is a condition "requiring a timely response"), and the Airbus lights-out rule ("no light = normal"). Equipment standards for a *technician at the unit* light "fine": the ODVA MS/NS pair (steady green = operational), Allen-Bradley RUN green beside FLT red, Cisco steady green = link, IBPI activity LED on when a drive is present. The green cell answers a question the dark board cannot: "was this checked and did it pass", as distinct from "not checked, unpowered, or dead LED". A field visit is the technician case. So: during the 3 s verdict window the grid should show the full stoplight – fine green cells beside amber and red – and go dark after it (4.1.1(a)). Boeing's "quiet and dark" is exactly this compromise: white confirms a normal selected position, then nothing.

**(b) Lamp test – yes, at boot, for free.** MIL-STD-411F 4.1.3.6 requires a single test switch for all dedicated displays, including blank growth positions; ODVA devices run a green/red 0.25 s alternation on power-up as a self-test (Rockwell 1834-UM001 "flashing green and red: power-up testing"). An all-cells-on sweep during the aux-steady boot phase satisfies both and costs no field attention. Without it, a dark cell is ambiguous between "fine" and "dead".

**(c) Blinking on the array – steady by default; if anything blinks, one rate, synchronised.** MIL-STD-411F 4.1.3.5: "flashing presentations should be avoided; however, where they are required for their alerting value … no more than two flash rates shall be used … flashing presentations which could be simultaneously active should have synchronized flashes." IBPI blinks only for *locate* (4 Hz) and *rebuild* (1 Hz), both transient. The status LED already carries the rhythm; a second, unsynchronised rhythm on the grid is noise.

**(d) Press-to-show in the field – yes; it is the dominant convention wherever power or attention is scarce.** Milwaukee fuel gauge: lights "will stay lit for two seconds" on press; EGO: "illuminated for 10 seconds"; Cisco's Mode button cycles STAT/DUPLX/SPEED; Tesla's charge-port light "turns off after a short period of time if no authenticated key is detected nearby" (owners report 1–2 min); AT&T TP-76208 R2-8 prefers a *remotely activated* blue unit indicator. Note the EGO exception: faults still show unprompted (five red flashing = low voltage, five red steady = over-temperature). The Margay 20 ms blip per row is the IBPI "activity" convention and IEC 60204-1 10.3.3 "flashing during transition"; it is defensible as a heartbeat. A button that replays the 3 s verdict is what the fuel-gauge convention would add.

**(e) Count codes versus rhythm codes – rhythm, with counts restricted to widely separated values.** Every embedded count code surveyed needs a card and draws miscounting reports (below). IALA E-110 limits counts to 3, 6+long and 9 for cardinal marks, rejects composite group-occulting "because it is difficult to recognize", forbids a single fixed light in the buoyage system, and asks for the fewest characters practicable. MIL-STD-411F caps flash rates at two. Margay's five rhythms follow the IALA model: shape-coded, colour carries severity. Three flags: (1) the 60/60 card-fault flutter (~8 Hz) exceeds IEC 60073's normal band (1.4–2.8 Hz, via IEEE P1621) and MIL-STD-411's 3–5 Hz, though 60073's own rule "higher frequency for higher priority" supports it being the fastest; (2) blue for READY is allowed as an advisory colour under 14 CFR 25.1322(e)(1)(iii) ("any color except red or green") but under IEC 60073/60204-1 blue means "mandatory action" and green means "normal" – the two standards disagree, and 25.1322 is the one written for a single-glance verdict; (3) ×8 pips versus ×3 pulses is a count distinction, and IALA would want it carried by shape too (it is: 100/300 versus 500/500).

## Count versus rhythm: the evidence

No controlled usability study of LED count codes was found (the only quantitative item, a blink-to-Morse paper reporting 62 % correctness and that SOS outperformed HELP, is an analogue at best). The documentary pattern is nonetheless consistent: every count-coded product (Pi, Particle, OBD-I, Dell, AMI) pairs its code with a lookup table and its forums with miscount threads, while the two institutions that designed for unaided recognition in the field – IALA and the military cockpit – restrict counts to maximally separated values (3/6+long/9), name rhythms by shape, and cap the number of distinct rates. Margay's protocol sits on the IALA side; its one count-like pair (×3 versus ×8) is already shape-separated.

## Where standards disagree with each other or with Margay

(1) Blue READY: permitted by 25.1322 (advisory), contradicted by IEC 60073/60204-1 (blue = action required, green = normal). (2) Flutter at ~8 Hz exceeds every cited rate band while obeying 60073's priority-ordering rule. (3) Fine-beside-fault: alarm/aviation says dark; PLC/network/storage says lit. The reconciling observation is that the lit camp serves a technician at the unit and the dark camp a monitor; a 3 s window followed by dark serves both. (4) Lamp test: MIL-STD-411 and ODVA require or provide one; Margay has none.
